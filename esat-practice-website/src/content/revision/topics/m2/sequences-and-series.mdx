## MM2.1 Sequences, nth-term formulae, and recurrence relations

A **sequence** is an ordered list of numbers; a **series** is the sum of a sequence. A sequence can be defined either by a formula for the $n$th term, or by a **recurrence relation** that builds each term from earlier ones, e.g. $x_{n+1}=f(x_n)$.

<Trap title="Don't assume a pattern from too few terms">
For $x_0=1,\ x_1=2,\ x_{n+2}=|x_n-x_{n+1}|$, the first four terms are $1,2,1,1$ — tempting to guess it repeats "$1,2,1$" forever. But each term depends on the *previous two* terms, so you need to see a repeated **pair** of adjacent terms before concluding the pattern repeats. Writing out more terms here actually gives $1,2,1,1,0,1,1,0,1,1,\ldots$ — a repeating block of 3, not 3 as first guessed.
</Trap>

<Shortcut title="Sum a periodic sequence in blocks">
If a recurrence produces a repeating block (e.g. every 3 terms), split the sum into full blocks plus a short leftover, and watch the ends carefully — $\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{100}x_n$ has $101$ terms, not 100, since it includes both endpoints (a classic "fence-post" counting slip).
</Shortcut>

## MM2.2 Arithmetic series

<FormulaCard title="Arithmetic progression (AP)">
$$
u_n = a+(n-1)d
$$

$$
S_n = \frac n2\bigl(2a+(n-1)d\bigr) = \frac n2(u_1+u_n) = n\times(\text{average term})
$$

where $a$ is the first term and $d=u_{n+1}-u_n$ is the common difference.
</FormulaCard>

<WorkedExample title="Sum to n terms">
Find the sum of the first 20 terms of $3,7,11,15,\ldots$

Here $a=3,\ d=4,\ n=20$:

$$
S_{20} = \frac{20}{2}\bigl(2(3)+19(4)\bigr) = 10(6+76) = 820
$$
</WorkedExample>

<Shortcut title="Sn = number of terms × average term">
$S_n=n\times\dfrac{u_1+u_n}{2}$ is often the fastest route: find the first and last term, average them, and multiply by how many terms there are — no need to recall the $2a+(n-1)d$ form at all.
</Shortcut>

Adding two arithmetic series term-by-term (or taking any linear combination $\alpha u_n+\beta v_n$) always produces another arithmetic series, with first term and common difference combined the same way.

<Shortcut title="Any AP term is the average of its neighbours">
For three consecutive terms of an AP, the middle one always equals the average of the other two: $u_n = \dfrac{u_{n-1}+u_{n+1}}{2}$. If a question gives you two terms either side of an unknown one, you can read it off directly without finding $a$ or $d$ first.
</Shortcut>

## MM2.3 Geometric series, and sum to infinity

<FormulaCard title="Geometric progression (GP)">
$$
u_n = ar^{n-1}, \qquad S_n = \frac{a(1-r^n)}{1-r}, \qquad S_\infty = \frac{a}{1-r} \ \ (\text{valid only for } |r|<1)
$$

The ratio of consecutive terms is constant: $\dfrac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}=r$.
</FormulaCard>

<WorkedExample title="Sum to infinity">
Find $S_\infty$ for $8,4,2,1,\ldots$

$$
a=8,\ r=\frac12 \ \Rightarrow\ S_\infty = \frac{8}{1-\frac12} = 16
$$
</WorkedExample>

<Trap title="Sum to infinity only exists when |r| < 1">
If $|r|\ge1$ the terms don't shrink to zero, so the series has no finite sum — always check $|r|<1$ before using $S_\infty=\dfrac{a}{1-r}$, especially when $r$ is given as an expression that could be $\ge1$ for some values.
</Trap>

<WorkedExample title="Summing a 'slice' of a GP (not the first n terms)">
For $S_k=a+ar+ar^2+\cdots+ar^{k-1}$, find $ar^m+ar^{m+1}+\cdots+ar^n$ (with $n>m$).

Factor out $ar^m$ and treat the bracket as a fresh GP starting at 1:

$$
ar^m+ar^{m+1}+\cdots+ar^n = ar^m\left(1+r+\cdots+r^{n-m}\right) = ar^m\times\frac{1-r^{n-m+1}}{1-r}
$$

Note there are $n-m+1$ terms here, **not** $n-m$ — count a small example on your fingers if unsure (e.g. $r^2$ to $r^4$ is 3 terms: $r^2,r^3,r^4$).
</WorkedExample>

<Shortcut title="New GPs from an old one">
Replacing $r$ by $-r$ in a GP gives another GP with the same first term and ratio $-r$. Taking every other term of a GP (e.g. $a+ar^2+ar^4+\cdots$) gives a GP with ratio $r^2$. Raising every term of a GP to a power $k$ gives a GP with first term $a^k$ and ratio $r^k$. Recognising these patterns turns "derived series" questions into direct substitutions into the standard formulae.
</Shortcut>

## MM2.4 The binomial expansion, n! and (n choose r)

<FormulaCard title="Binomial expansion">
$$
(a+f(x))^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}a^{n-k}\bigl[f(x)\bigr]^k, \qquad \binom{n}{r} = \frac{n!}{(n-r)!\,r!}
$$

The powers of the two terms in each piece of the expansion always add to $n$, and $\dbinom{n}{r}=\dbinom{n}{n-r}$.
</FormulaCard>

<WorkedExample title="Finding a single term without expanding everything">
Find the term in $x^7$ in $(3+2x)^8$.

Since we need $x^7$, that term must come from $(2x)^7$; the remaining power of $3$ must make the powers add to 8, so it's $3^1$. The binomial coefficient uses $n=8$ and either power, $r=7$ or $r=1$ (they're equal):

$$
\binom{8}{7}\,3^1(2x)^7 = 8\times3\times128\,x^7 = 3072x^7
$$
</WorkedExample>

<Trap title="Raise the whole term to the power, including its sign and coefficient">
For the coefficient of $x^5$ in $(2-3x)^7$, the term is $\dbinom{7}{5}2^2(-3x)^5$ — **not** $\dbinom{7}{5}2^2 3^5x^5$. Both the $-1$ and the $3$ inside $(-3x)$ must be raised to the power of 5, giving a negative coefficient. Forgetting the bracket around $-3x$ is the single most common binomial-expansion error.
</Trap>

<Shortcut title="Read off n, r, and the two powers directly from the target term">
For the $x^k$ term in $(a+bx)^n$: the power on $bx$ is $k$, the power on $a$ is $n-k$ (they must sum to $n$), and $\dbinom{n}{k}$ (or equivalently $\dbinom{n}{n-k}$) is the coefficient — no need to write out the general sigma formula each time.
</Shortcut>

<Shortcut title="Read small binomial coefficients straight off Pascal's triangle">
For $n$ up to about 8, it's faster to write out (or recall) the row of Pascal's triangle than to compute $\dfrac{n!}{(n-r)!\,r!}$ by hand: row $n=6$ is $1,6,15,20,15,6,1$, giving every $\binom6r$ at a glance.
</Shortcut>

<Tip title="(n choose r) counts unordered selections">
$\dbinom{n}{r}=\dfrac{n!}{(n-r)!\,r!}$ counts the number of ways to choose $r$ objects from $n$ without caring about order — $\dfrac{n!}{(n-r)!}$ counts *ordered* selections, and dividing by $r!$ removes the $r!$ different orderings of each selected group. This is also why $\dbinom nr=\dbinom{n}{n-r}$: choosing $r$ objects to keep is the same as choosing $n-r$ objects to discard.
</Tip>

## Formula and shortcut summary

- AP: $u_n=a+(n-1)d$, $S_n=\dfrac n2(u_1+u_n)$.
- GP: $u_n=ar^{n-1}$, $S_n=\dfrac{a(1-r^n)}{1-r}$, $S_\infty=\dfrac{a}{1-r}$ only when $|r|<1$.
- A "slice" $ar^m+\cdots+ar^n$ has $n-m+1$ terms — factor out $ar^m$ and treat the rest as a fresh GP.
- Binomial: $(a+f(x))^n=\sum\binom nk a^{n-k}[f(x)]^k$, with $\binom nr=\dfrac{n!}{(n-r)!\,r!}=\binom{n}{n-r}$.
- In a binomial term, raise the *entire* bracketed expression (sign, coefficient, and variable) to its power — never split it apart.
- Watch for fence-post errors: count terms/indices on a small example before trusting a formula's limits.
