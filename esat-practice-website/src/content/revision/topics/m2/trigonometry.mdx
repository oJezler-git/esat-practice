## MM4.1 The sine rule, cosine rule, and area of a triangle

Label a triangle with capital letters at the corners (angles) and matching lower-case letters on the sides opposite them.

<FormulaCard title="Area, sine rule, and cosine rule">
$$
\text{Area} = \frac12 ab\sin C
$$

$$
\frac{\sin A}{a} = \frac{\sin B}{b} = \frac{\sin C}{c} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C}
$$

$$
a^2 = b^2+c^2-2bc\cos A \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \cos A = \frac{b^2+c^2-a^2}{2bc}
$$
</FormulaCard>

The area formula $\frac12ab\sin C$ comes from base $a$ and vertical height $b\sin C$. Rotating which side is the "base" gives $\frac12ab\sin C=\frac12bc\sin A=\frac12ca\sin B$ — three equal expressions for the same area, which rearranges directly into the sine rule. The cosine rule is Pythagoras with a correction term for non-right angles; setting $A=90°$ recovers $a^2=b^2+c^2$ exactly.

| You're given | Use | Find |
|---|---|---|
| Two angles and one side | Sine rule | Any other side (third angle from angles summing to $180°$) |
| Two sides and a non-included angle | Sine rule | Another angle — **may be ambiguous** |
| Three sides | Cosine rule | Any angle |
| Two sides and the included angle | Cosine rule | The third side |

<Trap title="The ambiguous case: two sides and a non-included angle can give two triangles">
In triangle $ABC$, $A=30°$, $b=6$, $a=4$. The sine rule gives $\sin\theta=\dfrac{6\sin30°}{4}=\dfrac34$, so $\theta=48.6°$ **or** $\theta=180°-48.6°=131.4°$. Both are valid here since either, added to the known $30°$, stays under $180°$. Always check whether the obtuse solution is geometrically possible before discarding it — don't assume the calculator's answer is the only one.
</Trap>

<Shortcut title="Predict the ambiguous case before solving">
For two sides and a non-included angle, ambiguity (two possible triangles) can only happen when the given angle is acute *and* the side opposite it is shorter than the other given side. If the angle is obtuse or right, or the side opposite it is the longer one, there's only ever one valid triangle — check this before bothering to test the obtuse solution.
</Shortcut>

<WorkedExample title="Cosine rule for an angle from three sides">
A triangle has $a=7,\ b=8,\ c=5$. Find angle $A$.

$$
\cos A = \frac{b^2+c^2-a^2}{2bc} = \frac{64+25-49}{2(8)(5)} = \frac{40}{80} = \frac12 \ \Rightarrow\ A=60°
$$
</WorkedExample>

<Tip title="Right-angled triangle? Use Pythagoras and SOHCAHTOA, not the sine/cosine rules">
The sine and cosine rules always work, but if you can see a right angle, plain Pythagoras and basic trig ratios are faster and less error-prone — save the triangle rules for triangles that genuinely aren't right-angled.
</Tip>

## MM4.2 Radians, arc length, and sector area

A radian is the angle subtended by an arc equal in length to the radius. One full revolution is $2\pi$ radians $=360°$.

<FormulaCard title="Degree/radian conversion, arc length, sector area">
$$
\theta\text{ degrees} = \frac{\theta}{360}\times2\pi \text{ radians}, \qquad \alpha\text{ radians} = \frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\times360 \text{ degrees}
$$

For a sector of angle $\alpha$ radians in a circle of radius $r$:

$$
\text{arc length} = r\alpha, \qquad \text{area of sector} = \frac12 r^2\alpha
$$
</FormulaCard>

Both formulas fall out of "$\alpha$ radians is a $\dfrac{\alpha}{2\pi}$ fraction of the full circle": arc length $=2\pi r\times\dfrac{\alpha}{2\pi}=r\alpha$; sector area $=\pi r^2\times\dfrac{\alpha}{2\pi}=\frac12r^2\alpha$.

<WorkedExample title="Standard angle conversions">

| Degrees | $30°$ | $45°$ | $60°$ | $90°$ | $180°$ | $360°$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Radians | $\frac{\pi}{6}$ | $\frac{\pi}{4}$ | $\frac{\pi}{3}$ | $\frac{\pi}{2}$ | $\pi$ | $2\pi$ |

Learn this table directly rather than converting each time.
</WorkedExample>

<Trap title="Calculus identities like d/dx(sin x) = cos x only hold in radians">
Any question mixing trigonometry with differentiation or integration assumes the angle is in radians unless stated otherwise — working in degrees would introduce an extra constant factor and break the standard derivative/integral rules.
</Trap>

## MM4.3 Exact values for 0°, 30°, 45°, 60°, 90°

<WorkedExample title="Exact trig values from two standard triangles">
$45°$ comes from an isosceles right-angled triangle with legs 1 and hypotenuse $\sqrt2$. $30°$ and $60°$ come from bisecting an equilateral triangle of side 2, giving a right-angled triangle with sides $1,\sqrt3,2$.

| $\theta$ | $0°$ | $30°$ | $45°$ | $60°$ | $90°$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\sin\theta$ | $0$ | $\frac12$ | $\frac{\sqrt2}{2}$ | $\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$ | $1$ |
| $\cos\theta$ | $1$ | $\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$ | $\frac{\sqrt2}{2}$ | $\frac12$ | $0$ |
| $\tan\theta$ | $0$ | $\frac{1}{\sqrt3}$ | $1$ | $\sqrt3$ | undefined |
</WorkedExample>

## MM4.4 Graphs of sine, cosine, and tangent

$\sin$ and $\cos$ repeat every $360°$ ($2\pi$) and oscillate between $-1$ and $1$; $\tan$ repeats every $180°$ ($\pi$) and is undefined at $90°,270°,\ldots$ (where $\cos\theta=0$).

<Shortcut title="Think of sin and cos as projections">
Draw a line of fixed positive length at angle $\theta$ from the origin: $\cos\theta$ is its projection onto the $x$-axis, $\sin\theta$ its projection onto the $y$-axis. This is exactly the CAST diagram in disguise — it immediately tells you the sign of $\sin\theta$ and $\cos\theta$ in each quadrant without memorising a rule.
</Shortcut>

<WorkedExample title="Reading transformations off the graph">
For $y=\sin\left(2x+\dfrac{\pi}{6}\right)$: the coefficient 2 halves the period (compresses horizontally by factor 2), and the phase shift is found by solving $2x+\frac\pi6=0$, i.e. a shift of $-\frac{\pi}{12}$ — always factor out the coefficient of $x$ first to read off the shift correctly: $2\left(x+\frac{\pi}{12}\right)$.
</WorkedExample>

## MM4.5 $\tan\theta=\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}$ and $\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1$

<FormulaCard title="The core trig identities">
$$
\tan\theta = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}, \qquad \sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta \equiv 1
$$

Both hold for **every** angle $\theta$, not just acute ones in a right-angled triangle.
</FormulaCard>

$\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1$ is Pythagoras' theorem applied to $\sin\theta=\frac OH$ and $\cos\theta=\frac AH$ with $H=1$; $\tan\theta=\frac OA=\frac{O/H}{A/H}=\frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}$ follows immediately from the same two definitions.

<Shortcut title="Rearranged forms of the Pythagorean identity">
$\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta\equiv1$ rearranges to $\cos^2\theta\equiv1-\sin^2\theta$ or $\sin^2\theta\equiv1-\cos^2\theta$ — use whichever substitution turns a mixed sin/cos equation into a quadratic in a single function.
</Shortcut>

## MM4.6 Solving trigonometric equations

<WorkedExample title="Squared trig equation — find ALL solutions before rearranging">
Solve $\sin^2(2x+60°)=\dfrac14$ for $-360°<x<360°$.

Take the square root of both sides (both signs): $\sin(2x+60°)=\pm\frac12$.

For $\sin(2x+60°)=\frac12$: the basic solution is $2x+60°=30°$. **Before** isolating $x$, list every solution of $2x+60°$ that could map into range once divided by 2 and shifted by $-60°$:

$$
2x+60° = -690°,-570°,-330°,-210°,30°,150°,390°,510°,750°,870°
$$

Now solve for $x$ and keep only those inside $-360°<x<360°$:

$$
x = -315°,-195°,-135°,-15°,45°,165°,225°,345°
$$

Repeat for $\sin(2x+60°)=-\frac12$ to get the remaining solutions.
</WorkedExample>

<Trap title="Rearranging to x before listing all general solutions loses answers">
If you solve $2x+60°=30°\Rightarrow x=-15°$ first and *then* try to generate other solutions for $x$ by adding $360°$, you'll miss solutions — because the period of $2x+60°$ maps to a period of only $180°$ in $x$, not $360°$. Always list the general solutions in terms of the *original* argument, then divide/shift to isolate $x$ last.
</Trap>

<WorkedExample title="Converting a mixed sin/cos equation into a single-function quadratic">
Solve $12\cos^2x+6\sin x-10=2$ for $0°<x<360°$.

Substitute $\cos^2x=1-\sin^2x$, and let $S=\sin x$:

$$
12(1-S^2)+6S-10=2 \ \Rightarrow\ 12S^2-6S=0 \ \Rightarrow\ 6S(2S-1)=0
$$

$$
S=0 \text{ or } S=\frac12 \ \Rightarrow\ x = 30°,\ 150°,\ 180°
$$

(Note $x=0°$ and $x=360°$ are excluded by the strict inequality — always check the boundary against the given interval.)
</WorkedExample>

<Shortcut title="Estimate how many solutions to expect before you finish">
Roughly (range width) ÷ (period of the argument, converted back to $x$) tells you how many solutions to expect for each sign — for $\sin(2x+60°)=\pm\frac12$ over a $720°$ range in $x$, each half has a period of $180°$ in $x$, so expect around $4$ solutions per sign. If you end up with far fewer, you've likely dropped some at the boundary.
</Shortcut>

<Shortcut title="Spot which identity to substitute by looking for a lone sin or cos">
If an equation has $\cos^2x$ alongside a lone $\sin x$ term (or vice versa), substitute $\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1$ to eliminate whichever function appears only squared, leaving a normal quadratic in the other.
</Shortcut>

## Formula and shortcut summary

- Area: $\frac12ab\sin C$. Sine rule: $\dfrac{\sin A}{a}=\dfrac{\sin B}{b}=\dfrac{\sin C}{c}$. Cosine rule: $a^2=b^2+c^2-2bc\cos A$.
- Two sides + non-included angle (sine rule) can give **two** valid triangles — check both.
- Radians: arc length $=r\alpha$, sector area $=\frac12r^2\alpha$. $180°=\pi$ rad.
- Learn exact sin/cos/tan for $0°,30°,45°,60°,90°$ from the two standard triangles.
- $\tan\theta=\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}$, $\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta\equiv1$ — hold for every angle, not just acute ones.
- When solving $\sin(f(x))=k$ etc., list all general solutions for the *argument* first, then isolate $x$ last — rearranging too early loses solutions.
- Mixed $\sin$/$\cos$ equations: substitute the Pythagorean identity to get a quadratic in one function.
