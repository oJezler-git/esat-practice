## MM6.1 The derivative as a gradient and a rate of change

The **derivative** of $f(x)$ at a point is the gradient of the tangent to $y=f(x)$ there — how much $y$ changes per unit change in $x$. Gradient is just a rate of change: the gradient of a distance–time graph is speed, and the gradient of a speed–time graph is acceleration.

<FormulaCard title="Notation">
$$
\frac{dy}{dx}, \qquad \frac{d^2y}{dx^2}, \qquad f'(x), \qquad f''(x)
$$

$\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ (or $f'(x)$) is the **first derivative** — gradient of the tangent. $\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}$ (or $f''(x)$) is the **second derivative** — the derivative of the derivative, i.e. how the gradient itself is changing.
</FormulaCard>

<Tip title="Two different jobs, same expression">
$y=\ldots$ gives you a $y$-value for a given $x$-value. $\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\ldots$ gives you the *gradient* at that $x$-value. Keep straight which question you're actually being asked — "find the value" versus "find the gradient" call for different expressions.
</Tip>

## MM6.2 Differentiating powers of x

<FormulaCard title="Power rule">
$$
\frac{d}{dx}\bigl(x^n\bigr) = nx^{n-1} \qquad \text{for any rational } n
$$

Differentiate term by term: the derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives, and constants differentiate to $0$.
</FormulaCard>

The TMUA/ESAT tests only sums of powers of $x$ (including fractional and negative powers) — no chain rule, product rule, or trig differentiation. If an expression isn't already in that form, **simplify first**.

<WorkedExample title="Simplify before differentiating">
Differentiate $y=\dfrac{(3x+2)^2}{\sqrt x}$.

Expand the numerator and split the fraction into separate powers of $x$:

$$
y = \frac{9x^2+12x+4}{x^{1/2}} = 9x^{3/2}+12x^{1/2}+4x^{-1/2}
$$

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{27}{2}x^{1/2} + 6x^{-1/2} - 2x^{-3/2}
$$
</WorkedExample>

<Shortcut title="Term-by-term differentiation">
$\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^3+7x^2-3x+11) = 3x^2+14x-3$ — differentiate each term independently ($n x^{n-1}$) and drop any constant term entirely; there's no need to think of the expression as a single object.
</Shortcut>

## MM6.3 Tangents, normals, stationary points, and monotonicity

<FormulaCard title="Tangent and normal at a point">
At $x=a$, the tangent has gradient $f'(a)$ and the normal (perpendicular to the tangent) has gradient $-\dfrac{1}{f'(a)}$. Both pass through $(a,f(a))$, so:

$$
y - f(a) = f'(a)(x-a) \qquad \text{(tangent)}, \qquad y - f(a) = -\frac{1}{f'(a)}(x-a) \qquad \text{(normal)}
$$
</FormulaCard>

**Stationary points** occur where $\dfrac{dy}{dx}=0$ — the tangent is horizontal. To classify a stationary point at $x=a$, use the second derivative:

<FormulaCard title="Classifying stationary points">
$$
\frac{dy}{dx}=0 \text{ and } \frac{d^2y}{dx^2}>0 \ \Rightarrow\ \text{minimum}, \qquad \frac{dy}{dx}=0 \text{ and } \frac{d^2y}{dx^2}<0 \ \Rightarrow\ \text{maximum}
$$
</FormulaCard>

<WorkedExample title="Finding and classifying stationary points">
Find and classify the stationary points of $y=2x^3-3x^2-12x+6$.

$$
\frac{dy}{dx}=6x^2-6x-12=6(x-2)(x+1)=0 \ \Rightarrow\ x=2 \text{ or } x=-1
$$

$$
\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}=12x-6
$$

At $x=-1$: $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}=-18<0$, so a **maximum**. At $x=2$: $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}=18>0$, so a **minimum**. This also matches the shape of a positive cubic — maximum before minimum.
</WorkedExample>

<Shortcut title="A cubic's own shape tells you which stationary point is which">
For $y=ax^3+\ldots$ with $a>0$, the curve follows the classic "N" shape: it rises, turns at a **maximum**, falls, turns at a **minimum**, then rises again — so the *smaller*-$x$ stationary point is always the maximum and the *larger*-$x$ one the minimum. For $a<0$ it's reversed. This lets you sanity-check (or sometimes skip) the second-derivative test.
</Shortcut>

<Trap title="dy/dx = 0 and d²y/dx² = 0 is inconclusive">
The second-derivative test is **sufficient but not necessary**. At a stationary point, $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}>0$ guarantees a minimum and $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}<0$ guarantees a maximum — but $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}=0$ tells you nothing on its own. $y=x^4$ has a minimum at $x=0$ with $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}=0$ there; $y=-x^4$ has a maximum at $x=0$ with $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}=0$ there too. If the second derivative vanishes, check the sign of $\frac{dy}{dx}$ either side of the point instead.
</Trap>

<FormulaCard title="Strictly increasing and strictly decreasing">
$$
f'(x)>0 \text{ on } a<x<b \ \Rightarrow\ f \text{ strictly increasing on } a<x<b
$$

$$
f'(x)<0 \text{ on } a<x<b \ \Rightarrow\ f \text{ strictly decreasing on } a<x<b
$$
</FormulaCard>

<Trap title="These conditions are sufficient, not necessary">
$f'(x)>0$ everywhere guarantees $f$ is strictly increasing — but a function can be strictly increasing without $f'(x)>0$ at *every* point (e.g. $y=x^3$ is strictly increasing through $x=0$, yet $f'(0)=0$). Don't try to run the implication backwards.
</Trap>

<Tip title="Points of inflexion: qualitative only">
Points of inflexion (e.g. the flat-through point of $y=x^3$ at the origin) are not formally examined, but you should recognise where they can appear when sketching simple polynomials — typically where a stationary point "flattens through" rather than turning into a clean max or min.
</Tip>

## Formula and shortcut summary

- $\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^n)=nx^{n-1}$ for any rational $n$; differentiate sums term by term, constants vanish.
- Simplify an expression into a sum of powers of $x$ *before* differentiating — no chain or product rule is tested.
- Tangent at $x=a$: gradient $f'(a)$. Normal at $x=a$: gradient $-\dfrac{1}{f'(a)}$.
- Stationary points: solve $\dfrac{dy}{dx}=0$. Classify with $\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}$: positive $\Rightarrow$ min, negative $\Rightarrow$ max, zero $\Rightarrow$ inconclusive (check either side instead).
- $f'(x)>0$ on an interval $\Rightarrow$ strictly increasing there; $f'(x)<0$ $\Rightarrow$ strictly decreasing — sufficient conditions, not necessary ones.
